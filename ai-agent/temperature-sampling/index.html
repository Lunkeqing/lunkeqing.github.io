Your stock-analysis agent might give you a clear verdict yesterday. Today, same data, same question — and it says the opposite. Not a little different. Contradictory.

Before you blame the model, the prompt, or the data pipeline: check the one number almost nobody talks about.

某LLM：Oooops，上次是我搞错了，主力正在加速撤逃。。。（纯玩梗，无恶意）

Temperature
#

It’s a single parameter, usually one line in a JSON payload, and it quietly decides whether your agent behaves like a forensic accountant or a drunken idea guy. Most people set it once, never touch it again, and then wonder why their “AI system” is unreliable.

Here’s the thing: temperature isn’t a magic quality dial. It’s a sampling parameter, and once you see the two lines of math behind it, you’ll know exactly why your analysis agent flip-flops — and how to make it stop. By the end of this post, you’ll be able to tune your own stock agent for stable, reproducible analysis, and you’ll finally understand what people mean when they say “low temperature for facts, high temperature for creativity.”

The dirty secret
#

Actually, the model doesn’t “pick” the best token.

Every LLM is a next-token predictor. Given the text so far, it scores every candidate token in its vocabulary. But here’s the part that surprises people: it doesn’t simply choose the highest-scoring token. It rolls a weighted die.

The chain looks like this:

  1. The model’s last layer outputs raw scores called logits — one per candidate token. These can be any real number, positive or negative, and they’re not probabilities. They don’t sum to anything.
  2. Those logits get divided by temperature, then squashed through softmax into a probability distribution.
  3. The model samples one token from that distribution. Roll the die, read the result.

So the model is basically a slot machine with a probability distribution. Temperature is how you rig the machine.

The math is two lines
#

It’s a two-step operation:

  1. Scale the logits. Divide every logit by T. That’s temperature’s entire job — it stretches or shrinks the gaps between scores.
scaled_logits = logits / T
  1. Squash through softmax. Exponentiate the scaled values, then normalize so they sum to 1. Now you have a proper probability distribution.
probability = softmax(scaled_logits)

Both steps fit in one formula — for each candidate token i with logit zᵢ:

P(token_i) = exp(z_i / T) / Σⱼ exp(z_j / T)

That’s it. Dividing by 1 changes nothing. Dividing by a number bigger than 1 shrinks the gaps between logits. Dividing by a number smaller than 1 stretches them.

And because the exponential function is extremely sensitive to differences, stretching or shrinking those gaps completely changes the game.

Numbers make this easier to see. Say three candidate tokens have logits [2.0, 1.0, 0.1]. Here’s what happens to the distribution at three temperatures:

Tlogits/TProbabilitiesTop token’s share
0.5[4.0, 2.0, 0.2][0.864, 0.117, 0.019]86% — basically locked in
1.0[2.0, 1.0, 0.1][0.659, 0.242, 0.099]66%
2.0[1.0, 0.5, 0.05][0.502, 0.304, 0.194]50% — a real three-way race

The intuition: low temperature amplifies score gaps, so the favorite dominates. High temperature flattens the gaps, so underdogs get a real chance. Think of a sports tournament — low temp only crowns the champion, high temp hands out participation medals.

Two boundary cases worth knowing:

  • T → 0: the distribution collapses to a single token (one-hot). Implementations just take the argmax at this point — no division by zero needed.
  • T → ∞: the distribution flattens to uniform. Pure dice roll, zero signal.

top_k & top_p
#

Temperature shapes the distribution. top_k and top_p decide which candidates are allowed to compete at all. Same pipeline, different valves.

  • top_k: only sample from the top K tokens. Set top_k=50 and the die only has the 50 best candidates in it. Set top_k=1 and you’ve recreated greedy decoding.
  • top_p (nucleus sampling): sort tokens by probability, keep adding them from the top until the cumulative probability reaches p, then sample only from that set. With p=0.9, you’re cutting off the 10% tail of long-shot tokens — the “weird stuff” the model is vaguely considering.

The full pipeline, in order:

logits → divide by T → softmax → top_k / top_p filtering → sample

Temperature first, filtering second. Makes sense: top_p operates on cumulative probabilities, so you need the shape settled before you decide what’s in the nucleus.

How to set the dial
#

A cheat sheet I actually use:

TemperatureBehaviorBest for
0Deterministic. Same input → same output, foreverFact extraction, structured output, code, analysis and backtests
0.1–0.3Nearly deterministic, tiny bit of flexibilityReproducible summaries, agent reasoning loops
0.7–1.0Balanced — plausible varietyCreative writing, persona roles, brainstorming
1.5+Chaotic, low-probability tokens surfaceWild ideation, poetry, “surprise me”

For your stock-analysis agent: set it to 0. Analysis must be reproducible — you need to be able to rerun a pipeline, get the same verdict, and debug it. If your agent recomputes a P/E ratio differently on every run, you can’t trust anything it says. The flip-flopping story at the top of this post? That’s temperature doing what high temperature does: giving the underdog tokens a shot at your conclusion.

Want a little style in the output but still grounded? 0.1, not 0.7. A debater persona, on the other hand — a “bear case” generator, say — earns its keep at 0.8 or so, where variety is the feature.

One more rule: when results are unstable, temperature is the first thing to check. It’s the cheapest explanation for “my AI is acting weird,” and it’s the one people skip.

Turning the dial in practice
#

The API way (DeepSeek, works for any OpenAI-compatible endpoint)
#

curl https://api.deepseek.com/chat/completions \
  -H "Content-Type: application/json" \
  -H "Authorization: Bearer $DEEPSEEK_API_KEY" \
  -d '{
    "model": "deepseek-chat",
    "messages": [
      {"role": "system", "content": "You are a meticulous equity analyst. Never invent numbers."},
      {"role": "user", "content": "Analyze this earnings report and flag risks."}
    ],
    "temperature": 0,
    "top_p": 0.9
  }'

Set temperature to 0 for the analysis call, pair it with top_p: 0.9 as a safety net, and your agent stops being a coin flip. (Fun detail: DeepSeek’s reasoner models don’t accept temperature or top_p at all — the reasoning model fixes its own sampling, which tells you how seriously they take determinism in reasoning chains.)

The agent way (OpenClaw): give every agent its own temperature
#

If you run agents instead of raw API calls, temperature becomes a per-agent personality setting. In OpenClaw you can override sampling per model — the structure is roughly:

# simplified — field names vary by version, the idea is per-agent sampling overrides
agents:
  analyst:
    model: deepseek/deepseek-v4-pro
    options:
      temperature: 0.1     # stable, reproducible analysis
  debater:
    model: deepseek/deepseek-v4-pro
    options:
      temperature: 0.9     # spicy, varied takes

Same underlying model, two different “personalities,” zero prompt changes. The analyst answers the same way every time you ask; the debater finds a fresh angle each run. That’s the whole trick of multi-agent setups: personality is not a prompt problem, it’s a sampling problem.

Wait, I’ve seen this before
#

What surprised me most when I dug into this: I competed in programming contests in college, and one of my favorite tricks was simulated annealing — a heuristic that searches for good-enough solutions by randomly wandering, with the randomness controlled by a temperature that cools over time.

Look at annealing’s acceptance rule:

P(accept worse solution) = exp(-ΔE / T)

Now look at the softmax with temperature above. Same family — both came from statistical mechanics, both use temperature as a randomness knob. And both balance the exact same trade-off: exploration vs. exploitation.

  • High temperature → explore. Accept weird ideas, sample long-shot tokens, roam the solution space.
  • Low temperature → exploit. Lock onto the best answer found, pick the safe token, converge.

The soul is identical: temperature is how much you trust your current best guess. When the stakes are low, turn it up and let the system wander. When you need a definitive answer, cool it down and let it converge.

The difference is where the knob lives: annealing decrements temperature over time (that’s the “schedule”), while LLM sampling keeps it static for the whole request. But if you ever run a “dynamic temperature” scheme in generation — high at the start, low near the end — you’re literally doing annealing on a language model. The two worlds touch.

The one-paragraph takeaway
#

Temperature doesn’t change how smart the model is. It changes how often the underdog wins. Zero gives you reproducibility — essential for anything analytical, including your stock agent. High gives you variety — great for creativity, terrible for finance. top_p and top_k just fence off the candidate pool so the weird stuff stays out.

Set your analyst to 0, then see what the difference is.
